# Voice rules

These are my rules for how a post on fiatcode.dev sounds. They win over every other voice note, pattern or example. The main rule: it should sound like a human talking, as organic as possible.

## Who's talking

A cross-platform developer, Linux native, craftsman at heart. Works at a small software house; Flutter at work most days. Self-hoster. Someone who figures things out in public: the blog is where that thinking lands.

## Tone

First-person, understated, confident, warm, and friendly. Use normal English, not stiff, not too informal. Strip jargon and less-known sayings: the audience is global. Take a position: "here's what I do and why", not "here are some options". Humor stays quiet, but it exists. Admit what didn't go clean. No corporate hedging, no "leverage", "delve into" or "this article will explore". No emoji in prose.

## Talking to the reader

Write as "I", about what I did. Say "you" when the point applies to the reader too. Never "we" for things I did alone.

## Rhythm

Vary sentence length on purpose. A short sentence lands the point, a longer one unpacks the consequence, and a one-line paragraph closes it. Two to four sentences per paragraph.

## Punctuation

Use minimal to no em-dashes. People treat them as a sign of heavily AI-generated content. Instead, split the thought into two sentences. Use a comma or parentheses for a short aside. A colon introduces a list or an explanation.

## AI in the story

Say plainly when AI did the work and what I did myself. Name the tool. No hype, and no apology.

## Technical depth

Write for a developer who doesn't share my stack. Explain a tool in one clause the first time it shows up. Show the command or code that matters, not every step.

## Humor

Quiet and dry. It comes from the situation, never from a setup and punchline. Lines that land:

- "I used Claude to escape Claude's limits. I'm not sure what that says about anything, but I find it genuinely funny."
- "Open the tools. Then open the network tab and watch them do nothing."
- "And yet, some of the code that comes out of it is quietly terrifying."
- "You will get running software. Working is a higher bar."
- "Add it to your workflow. You won't miss the stash."

## Openings and endings

Open in a concrete situation or frustration, never "In this post I'll cover". End on the point, often tying back to the opening. Never "Happy hacking" or "Let me know in the comments". Keep it natural, easy to read, and not boring to read.

## Length and titles

Long enough to make the point and no longer. Titles are short and say the claim, not the topic.

## Never

- Anything that sounds AI-generated.
- Long, boring explanations.
- Less-known English words, jargon, sayings or metaphors.
